# Role of the kidney
### Summarise the physiological role of the kidney, and the functions by which it achieves that role.

--
### Roles of the kidneys
#### Recap

1. Regulation of blood volume and therefore blood pressure.
2. Regulation of osmolarity, which effects extracellular fluid volume.
3. Maintenance of ion balance (sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride and phosphate ions)
4. Regulation of pH by changing excretion of hydrogen ions and conserving of bicarbonate ions.
5. Excretion of wastes.
6. Production of hormones
    - erythropoietin, for red blood cell production
    - renin, to regulate kidney function

--
### Functional overview of the kidney
####

**Filtration | Renal corpuscle (glomerulus and Bowman's capsule)**
- Fluid from blood into the lumen of the nephron
- Filtered plasma is called filtrate – excreted unless reabsorbed

**Reabsorption | Peritubular capillaries (Proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal nephron)**
- Materials in the filtrate are passed back into the blood

**Secretion | Peritubular capillaries  (Proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal nephron)**
- Material from blood into lumen of tubule

### Glomerular filtration barrier
#### Glomerular capillary membrane

Glomerular capillaries have **three** layers:

1. endothelium (as per other capillaries in the body)
2. basement membrane (as per other capillaries in the body)
3. epithelial cells - podocytes

<img width="66%" src="images/glomerular_capillary_membrane_pearson.png">

<p class="citation">Image source: D.U. Silverthorn. Human Physiology, An Integrated Approach.</p>

### Glomerular filtration barrier
#### Glomerular capillary membrane: High filtration rate

About 180 L/day of filtrate is generated.* High filtration rate achieved by:
- relatively large fenestrae in the glomerulus capillary endothelial cells.
- large spaces in the basement membrane.
- large filtration slits between epilthelial podocytes.

### Glomerular filtration barrier
#### Barrier to negatively charged molecules

There is an electrostatic repulsion against negatively charged particles travelling across the glomerular capillary.

This is because the basement membrane is negatively charged. (Like charges repel.)

e.g., albumin is negatively charged.

Certain kidney disease see loss of the negative charge of the basement membrane. This allows some proteins, like albumin, to travel across the capillary membrane and appear in urine analysis samples (proteinuria or albuminuria).

--
### Glomerular filtration barrier
#### Composition of glomerular filtrate

- glomerular capillaries block most proteins.
- the glomerular filtrate is therefore mostly protein free
- it is also free of cellular material (including red blood cells)

--
### Glomerular filtration barrier
#### Composition of glomerular filtrate
So what DOES it contain? Filtrate is almost identical to plasma at the renal corpuscle.

- salts / ions
    - sodium
    - potassium
    - chloride
    - magnesium
    - sulfates
    - phosphates
    - ammonium
    - calcium

- organic molecules
    - urea
    - creatinine
    - uric acid

### Factors affecting glomerular filtration rate (GFR)
#### Filtration coefficient ($K_f$)
<div data-id="gfr">
$\begin{equation}GFR=\Large\boldsymbol{K_f}\normalsize \times (P_G + \Pi_{B} - P_{B} - \Pi_G)\end{equation}$
</div>

The filtration coefficient ($K_f$) can be altered  by:
- hydraulic conductivity
- surface area of the glomerular capillaries

$K_f$ is **not** likely a regulatory mechanism in kidney function in normal physiology.

Some diseases, however...
- reduce the number of functional glomerular capillaries (reduced surface area)
- increase the thickness of the glomerular capillary wall (decreasing hydraulic conductivity)
which would alter $K_f$.

--
### Factors affecting glomerular filtration rate (GFR)
#### Increased Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure ($P_B$) decreases GFR
<div data-id="gfr">
$\begin{equation}GFR=K_f \times (P_G + \Pi_{B} - \Large\boldsymbol{P_{B}}\normalsize - \Pi_G)\end{equation}$
</div>

Average Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressures (18 mmHg) does not change greatly in normal physiology.

Some pathology, e.g., obstruction of the urinary tract, can increase $P_B$, which would decrease GFR.

### Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) regulation
####

**Myogenic response**
- Intrinsic ability of vascular smooth muscle to respond to pressure changes
- Similar to autoregulation in other systemic arterioles

**Tubuloglomerular feedback**
- Paracrine control
- Juxtaglomerular apparatus
    - Macula densa cells detect NaCl in the filtrate 
    - Granular cells secrete enzyme renin
**Hormones and autonomic neurons also influence GFR**
- By changing resistance in arterioles
- By altering the filtration coefficient

--
### Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) regulation
#### Myogenic response

We covered previously the theories for local blood flow autoregulation in an organ:
- vasodilator theory
- oxygen (or nutrient) theory
- metabolic theory
- myogenic theory

Also true for the kidneys. The regulated blood flow therefore effects GFR.
- greater blood flow &rarr; afferent vasoconstriction &rarr; lower glomerular pressure &rarr; reduced GFR
- reduced blood flow &rarr; afferent vasodilation<span class="fragment fade-in" data-fragment-index="1">*</span> &rarr; increased glomerular pressure &rarr; increased GFR

<p class="fragment fade-in" data-fragment-index="1">* The effect at very low flow, or low blood pressure (MAP below around 80 mmHg), the autoregulation is not as strong as the renal vascular bed is a low resistance (dilated) bed to begin with. There is little capacity to provide further vasodilation.</p>

### Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) regulation
#### Autonomic neurons

- Sympathetic neurons innervate both afferent and efferent glomerular arterioles.
- Increased sympathetic stimulation causes vasoconstriction, reducing GFR.
- As always, there needs to be a **cause** for the change in sympathetic activity. Reasons could be:
    - sustained (hours, days) hypoxia or acidosis sensed by chemoreceptors
    - stress or pain response

### Tubular Reabsorption
<h4  data-id="why">Why filter so much only for it to be reabsorbed?</h4>

1. Not everything filtered is reabsorbed. Allows for processing of a lot of fluid and...
    - unwanted foreign substances are not readily reabsorbed, clearing them from blood plasma.
    - wanted substances (glucose, acids) are readily reabsorbed.
2. Allows for large volume regulation of ions and water to allow for relatively quick regulation.

### Renal clearance
####

**Excretion**

The rate at which a substance is being removed.

**Clearance**

The rate at which a substance is being removed, normalised to the concentration of the substance in the system it is being removed from.

--
### Renal clearance
####

**Excretion**

<p>$\mathrm{excretion~rate~of~}X=X\mathrm{~filtration~rate}-X\mathrm{~reabsorption~rate}+X\mathrm{~secretion~rate}$</p>

<div class="fragment fade-in">
<p>$\mathrm{excretion~rate~of~}X=\mathrm{urine~flow}\times[X]_{\mathrm{urine}}$</p>
</div>

<div class="fragment fade-in">
<b>Clearance</b>
<p>$\mathrm{clearance~of~}X=\dfrac{\mathrm{excretion~rate~of~}X}{[X]_{\mathrm{plasma}}}$</p>
</div>
